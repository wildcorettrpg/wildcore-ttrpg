# Multi-Race Characters

## Design Intent

Multi-race characters support the **Get Out of the Way** principle: if a player imagines their character as Faerie/Shapeshifter, or Elf/Dwarf, the system should enable that vision rather than obstruct it. The mechanics are deliberately simple at the table — the only complexity occurs at character creation, which is an acceptable exception to the **No Unnecessary Math** rule.

## Character Creation

### Selecting Races

A player designing a multi-race character selects two races: a **primary race** and a **secondary race**. The player freely designates which is primary. This choice determines the character's total trait and flaw count — a multi-race character has as many traits and racial flaws as their primary race.

### Racial Swaps

A multi-race character begins with all traits and racial flaws from their primary race. They then perform **racial swaps**: each swap replaces one primary race trait and one primary race flaw with one trait and one flaw from the secondary race.

At least one swap is required. Players may make as many additional swaps as they wish, limited only by what the secondary race has available.

Mandatory traits and flaws from either race are not part of the swap pool — they are always present and cannot be replaced.

### Natural Limits

The secondary race's available pool determines how far a player can swap. A secondary race with three non-mandatory traits and three non-mandatory flaws allows a maximum of three swaps.

## Racial Skill Trees

Multi-race characters have access to **both** racial skill trees — one for each of their races. Pure-race characters have access only to their own racial skill tree.

Racial skill trees allow characters to reduce and eventually eliminate racial flaws through staged skill point investment. Each flaw has multiple stages of reduction; each stage requires a separate skill purchase. A character who chooses not to invest in flaw reduction keeps those flaws indefinitely — the choice not to invest is as meaningful as the choice to invest.

### Why Two Trees Matter

Access to two racial skill trees is the long-term payoff of the multi-race choice. A pure-race character can go deeper into a single tree; a multi-race character has more options but must make real choices about where to invest limited skill points. Neither is strictly better — they represent different kinds of growth.

A mixed-race character can buy off flaws from either race by investing in the corresponding racial tree. A flaw taken from the secondary race during creation can be reduced or eliminated through secondary race tree investment.

### Tree Structure

Racial skill trees branch. The trunk contains positive racial abilities that can be developed beyond the base traits — these progress linearly. Each flaw has its own independent branch off that trunk. Players invest in whichever branches address the flaws they want to reduce, and ignore the rest. No player should be forced to spend skill points eliminating a flaw they want to keep.

### Branch Types

Flaw branches come in two types:

**Open branches** are available for skill point investment at any time. These cover behavioral flaws — characteristics a character can consciously work on without requiring a specific triggering experience. A Dwarf working on Rabble-rouser doesn't need the story to provide a particular situation; they can simply decide to become more measured.

**Experience-gated branches** require a narrative milestone before any skill points can be spent. These cover environmental flaws — limitations that can only be overcome through direct exposure to the thing that causes them. A Dwarf cannot buy off Greenhorn without having spent meaningful time at sea. A Dwarf cannot buy off Claustrophobia without having faced and survived enclosed spaces. The story must happen first; the skill point investment ratifies what the character lived through.

Narrative milestones are determined by the Narrator in collaboration with the player. A player who wants to work on an experience-gated flaw signals that intent; the Narrator ensures the story creates the opportunity. The opportunity becomes part of the character's story arc.

### Why This Matters

Experience-gated branches make the Narrator a meaningful participant in character development. A sea voyage isn't just a travel montage — it's a potential turning point for a Dwarf with Greenhorn. The Narrator knows that, and can use it deliberately. When the skill point is finally spent, it carries the weight of everything that earned it.

## Design Notes

- Multi-race characters start with the same total number of traits as their primary race — they are not more powerful at creation, only different.
- The minimum one swap ensures the secondary heritage is always mechanically present, not merely cosmetic.
- Each swap always moves a trait and a flaw together, preventing players from taking secondary race benefits without also taking secondary race costs.
- The staged flaw buyoff model means flaws are a starting portrait, not a permanent sentence. Characters grow by becoming less constrained, not only by becoming more capable.
